# Failure as Fuel: How a Crisis Narrative Converts Policy Breakdown into Mandate

- Course: [Demagoguery 101](./index.md)
- Layer: 1 / run 001
- Intent: Foundations — definitions, surface examples, why the topic matters

## Source

[The worse it gets, the better it is: American leftism is thriving on its own failures](https://thehill.com/opinion/campaign/6030675-american-left-politics-failure/)

## Lesson

Demagoguery, at this foundation, is not merely insult or crowd-pleasing. It is a public argument that treats a policy’s own wreckage as proof that the policy must be enlarged. The speaker does not deny the wreckage. The speaker harvests it.

Jonathan Turley’s column names the harvest: “nothing succeeds like failure.” Immigration, health insurance, urban schooling, transit fares, rent, and policing are presented as domains where left-wing programs produce worse numbers, then those numbers become the warrant for more radical versions of the same programs. The mechanism is sequential. First, a promised result is stated (Obama’s 2008 claim that premiums would fall by up to $2,500 per family per year). Second, the opposite result is recorded (premiums nearly tripled after the Affordable Care Act; deductibles more than doubled; family coverage costs rose by more than $10,000; median premiums rose 20 percent in 2026 and another 15 percent in 2027; enrollments fell). Third, the remedy offered is not reversal but intensification: more regulation and “Medicare for all.”

The same three-beat pattern appears on the border. After an administration “chose to allow millions of undocumented people to enter,” Democrats shift to “pathways” to citizenship on the ground that the population is now too large to deport. Turley adds a contrast case: the subsequent administration “immediately stopped crossings using the same laws Biden had refused to enforce.” The point for this course is not to settle that empirical claim. The point is the rhetorical use of irreversibility: once the fact on the ground exists, the fact is treated as a moral veto on enforcement.

Zohran Mamdani is Turley’s local specimen of the same loop. A promise of free buses produces fare-skipping, including by affluent riders, and tens of millions in Metropolitan Transportation Authority losses; the losses then justify larger subsidies or permanent fare abolition. Promised rent control is said to trigger preemptive rent hikes (average apartment $4,965; Manhattan $6,655), which then justify still tighter controls. Hostility toward police, including being booed off a law-enforcement stage, coincides with NYPD attrition of as many as 300 officers a month—downsizing achieved by exit rather than statute. Taxes and rhetoric that the affluent are not “paying their fair share” are said to produce flight of taxpayers and jobs, which then tightens the fiscal vise and the demand for more extraction.

A second, quieter mechanism is attrition of the opposing class. Tracy Rosenthal, co-author of *Abolish Rent*, is cited as treating rent as “a ransom” and using complaint cascades and enforcement programs so that landlords “surrender” property. Turley reads Mamdani’s tenant-coordination offices as the same squeeze: failure of private rental supply becomes the pretext for tenant or nonprofit ownership. Government, unlike a firm that shrinks when it sells less for more, “is the only industry that grows with failure.”

Historically, Turley places the pattern in twentieth-century Britain and France, where socialist policy is said to have produced unemployment, subsidy demand, and finally emergency expansion of the state (Britain’s IMF rescue). He quotes Lenin’s instruction to “utilize” crisis to “hasten the collapse of the rule of capital.” For a first module, retain only the structural claim: upheaval is framed as opportunity, not tragedy.

What matters for demagoguery is the inversion of ordinary accountability. In a market test, shrinking quality and rising price destroy the seller. In this political test, shrinking quality and rising price enlarge the seller’s jurisdiction. The audience is invited to treat each worse metric as a moral accusation against opponents and as a technical reason to deepen the program that generated the metric.

## Key concepts in this module

- **Failure-as-warrant**: using a program’s bad outcomes as the reason to expand the program.
- **Irreversibility argument**: “too many to reverse” (deportations, fare norms, rental stock) as a substitute for justification.
- **Preemptive response**: actors (landlords, fare-payers, taxpayers, officers) changing behavior in anticipation of the next rule, which then “proves” the next rule.
- **Attrition politics**: achieving downsizing of police, landlords, or taxpayers by exit rather than explicit repeal.
- **State as non-firm**: an institution that can grow when its product worsens, because it taxes rather than sells.
- **Crisis utilization**: treating social and economic breakdown as a resource for further control (Lenin cited as the extreme statement of the habit).

## Structural tensions

- Promised cost reduction versus recorded premium, deductible, and enrollment deterioration, with intensification as the official reply.
- Open-entry policy versus later claim that scale forbids enforcement.
- Rent-control threat versus rising asking rents, used to justify more control.
- Fare abolition talk versus fare evasion and subsidy demand.
- Anti-police posture versus officer flight that delivers the downsizing the posture wanted.

## Open questions

- When is “doubling down after failure” sincere belief, coalition payoff (unions, tenant groups), or conscious crisis utilization?
- How much of the cited price and migration movement is caused by the named policies versus national rates?
- What audience filters make failure feel like proof rather than falsification?
- Does the same failure-as-warrant pattern appear on the right, or is this module only mapping one coalition’s version?

## Spine update

The course begins with a single spine: demagoguery as the public conversion of policy failure into a larger mandate. Turley’s 2026 Hill column supplies the first working diagram—immigration scale used to block deportation; ACA premium and deductible spikes used to argue Medicare for all; urban school failure used to demand more budget and less choice; Mamdani’s fare, rent, tax, and policing fights used to show preemptive private responses that then “require” more state; Rosenthal-style tenant enforcement used to transfer control by complaint load; historical IMF-Britain and Lenin’s crisis line used to claim that radicals prefer the wreckage. Known so far: the three-beat sequence (promise, contrary metric, intensification) and the claim that government can grow when a firm would die. Not yet known: motives, counterexamples, or the psychology of the crowd that accepts the inversion.
